\section{The Problem: One Address, Many Different Relationships}

A person gives an email address to a hospital, an insurer, a pharmacy and many
other organizations. Correspondence from all of them reaches the same entrance.
When the address is circulated, the recipient has little context about which
relationship supplied that route. Replacing it can disrupt unrelated contacts,
portal access and account notices.

Expected healthcare correspondence also comes from people the recipient has not
contacted before. A hospital may pass contact details to a specialist, and its
departments or service vendors may use different sending addresses. Requiring
each correspondent to make a paid approach would put the request process in the
way of an existing organizational relationship.

A publicly listed address serves a different purpose. A professor may welcome
new research proposals while needing control over unsolicited approaches. The
existing bonded request gives a stranger an explicit way to ask for permission.
Both uses belong in CS-MAIL, within the same person's account.

\section{A Private Alias for a Hospital}

The member creates a private alias for their hospital and gives it during
registration. Creation automatically opens an \textbf{alias-scoped express lane}.
The hospital can send ordinary email through it without a bonded request or
adopting the CS-MAIL application.

Later, a specialist whom the member has never contacted receives that alias
through the hospital relationship. The specialist can write through the same
lane, subject to its admission policy, without first receiving a separate
sender-scoped grant. The member sees which alias received the correspondence
alongside the available evidence about its author.

The permission is confined to that receiving alias. Using the hospital alias
does not establish permission through the member's insurer alias or public
address. The same account can manage these routes without creating another
account, product login or membership.

There is no bonded route through the private alias. Restricting or retiring it
does not turn an organization's next email into a payment invitation. What happens
to affected correspondence, and how replacement preserves continuity, requires
an explicit lifecycle policy before implementation.

\section{A Public Address for Professor Bob}

Professor Bob publishes an address for professional approaches. Creating this
public-facing alias grants no express lane. Alice, who lacks permission, selects
one of Bob's published request classes and authorizes its processing component
and collateral under the existing protocol.

Bob can accept with standing permission or explicitly grant a
\textbf{sender-scoped express lane}. Either form of request acceptance records
the full refund obligation. Rejection and expiry retain their existing financial
consequences. Subsequent communication follows the permission granted; the lane
does not charge for each message. Other existing permissions, including scoped
member-initiated invitations, retain their own rules.

Public-facing aliases are the primary entry point for bonded approaches. The
private-alias design does not change request pricing, settlement, annual service
charges or equal member distributions.

\section{Two Bases for Express-Lane Permission}

\paragraph{Alias-scoped.}
The member authorizes a route by creating a private alias. Permission is bound
to that receiving address and its active lane policy. It can accommodate a new
sender who has been given the address without requiring individual approval first.

\paragraph{Sender-scoped.}
The member explicitly authorizes a sender subject under a grant's scope and
validity. Existing lanes may bind to a native identity, an external mailbox or a
recipient-selected legacy domain. The existing
\href{cs_mail_express_lanes.tex}{express-lane memo} explains this model, and its
\href{cs_mail_express_lanes_implementation.tex}{implementation plan} records the
current sender-scoped machinery.

An alias is a communication address and managed route; a lane is permission.
The automatic association between a private alias and its lane does not make
addresses interchangeable with accounts, sender identities or consent grants.
Standing acceptance remains a distinct permission. The mapping between aliases
and protocol identities is still open.

\section{What the Route Tells the Member}

The hospital alias identifies the entrance the member supplied for that
relationship. It does not prove that every message was authored by the hospital,
that every new sender is its authorized delegate, or that the hospital caused an
address leak. A private alias can be forwarded or disclosed. Sender authentication
supplies separate evidence about the message's origin.

The design therefore distinguishes route, evidence and permission. A previously
unknown sender is not automatically excluded merely for lacking a sender-scoped
grant, but the handling of failed authentication, suspicious messages and other
refusals still needs an agreed policy. This memo does not choose delivery,
quarantine or rejection for those cases, nor promise that other clinical-data
channels will compensate for a missed email.

External email retains its existing privacy boundaries: receiving and relay
services may handle plaintext. The alias does not confer endpoint-only
confidentiality. Existing disclosure-consent requirements remain applicable;
the alias design does not itself authorize outbound disclosure or managed decryption.

\section{CS-MAIL Owns the Infrastructure; Phoros Uses It Later}

CS-MAIL, operated by C-SQD, PBC, owns alias creation, routing, permissions,
lifecycle management and correspondence. The member must be able to use these
capabilities independently of Phoros.

Phoros Account, operated by Reservatory, PBC, will later use the infrastructure
through a linked CS-MAIL account. It can associate healthcare relationships with
their communication routes without building a second alias system. Product
linking does not itself grant access to messages, authorize decryption or disclose
healthcare records. The \href{docs/product-identity-model.md}{product identity
model} continues to distinguish product accounts, logins and shared subject
association. No primary recovery email address is introduced by this design.

\section{Decisions Still Required}

The two permission scopes and CS-MAIL ownership are agreed. The following remain
open and must not be filled in by borrowing defaults from sender-scoped lanes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lane lifetime, renewal and whether private routes expire automatically.
  \item Restriction and block scope, including their interaction across aliases.
  \item Authentication requirements and handling of suspicious or refused mail.
  \item Replacement and retirement, including portal login, recovery messages,
  notices and any transition or quarantine period.
  \item Outbound address selection and continuity of replies and conversations.
  \item Alias-to-protocol-identity mapping and the grant, storage and wire contracts.
  \item Milestone placement, member-management flows and validation scope.
\end{itemize}

Creating an alias-scoped lane must not be assumed to settle an existing paid
request. Existing sender-scoped grants and their settlement semantics remain
unchanged. This document records product direction; it does not assert that the
current SMTP receiver already admits correspondence through private aliases.
